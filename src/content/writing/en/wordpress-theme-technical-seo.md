The catalogue site for the NFC products business I run started on a purchased
theme. That was the right call at the time — it got a working shop online in
days instead of weeks.

It stopped being the right call about a year later, and the reason wasn't
performance or design. It was that every decision I needed to make at the search
level had to be made *against* the theme rather than with it.

## What "fighting the theme" actually means

Commercial themes are built to be configurable for the widest possible range of
buyers. That generality has a cost, and the cost lands squarely on technical SEO.

**URL structure was assembled in three places.** Permalink settings, a taxonomy
prefix in the theme options, and a filter buried in a bundled plugin. Changing a
product URL pattern meant finding all three and hoping nothing else read them.

**Near-duplicate pages were generated by default.** Filter and sort parameters
each produced a crawlable URL. Colour, size, sort order — every combination was
a distinct page as far as a crawler was concerned, all serving substantially the
same content. The theme had no notion that these shouldn't be indexed, because
from its point of view they're a feature.

**Template metadata was hardcoded in the wrong layer.** Title patterns lived
inside template files. Meaning: editing a title tag meant editing PHP, and any
theme update overwrote it.

**Redirects were somebody else's plugin.** A separate plugin with its own
database table and its own idea of precedence. When a redirect didn't fire, the
question "who is handling this URL" had no single answer.

None of these are bugs. They're what generality looks like from the inside.

## What I built instead

A standalone theme, with three things that were previously scattered pulled into
one place.

**Redirect management as part of the theme.** One table, one owner, one place to
look when a URL doesn't behave. Redirects are the sort of thing where ambiguity
about ownership is the whole problem.

**Explicit indexing controls.** Faceted URLs are non-indexable by default rather
than by exception. The rule is inverted: a parameterised URL has to earn its way
into the index, instead of being crawlable until someone notices and blocks it.

**A consistent product-page structure.** Product content is data — fields with
defined roles — rather than free-form HTML pasted into an editor. This is the
change that mattered most and it isn't really an SEO change at all. It means
product pages are structurally identical, so structured data can be generated
from real fields, and a template change applies everywhere at once instead of
requiring five hundred manual edits.

## The part I got wrong first

My first version put too much logic in templates. It worked, and it recreated
the original problem in a new place: content decisions living inside
presentation code.

The second pass separated them properly. Templates render. Data lives in fields.
The rule I ended up with: if changing it requires touching PHP, it's in the wrong
layer.

That single constraint is most of what makes the site maintainable now. Product
content can be edited without touching application logic — which sounds like a
small thing until you consider that the alternative is a developer in the loop
for every copy change.

## Is this worth it for you?

Honestly: often not.

Replacing a working theme is a real cost, and for a small catalogue it isn't
justified. The purchased theme was the correct decision for the first year and I
would make it again.

The signal that it stopped being correct was specific and worth naming: **I was
regularly unable to answer "why is this URL indexed?" without reading plugin
source.** When the platform stops being able to explain its own behaviour to you,
configuration has run out and you're now maintaining someone else's assumptions.

That's the threshold. Not page speed, not design — whether you can still reason
about what the site is telling search engines.
