ШЛЮЗ is a browser horror game about checking documents at an airlock 340 metres
underwater. It contains no audio files. Every sound — the hull hum, radio static,
sonar pings, knocking, the operator's own breathing — is generated by oscillators
and filters at runtime.

The constraint started as practicality: a browser game that has to load fast, and
horror audio is otherwise the heaviest thing you ship. What I didn't anticipate
was that synthesis would change what the audio could *do*.

## The room tone is two sine waves and a filter

The base layer is the sound of being inside a metal structure under pressure. In
practice that's two low sine oscillators — around 40 Hz and 63 Hz — summed and
run through a lowpass filter.

Two frequencies rather than one matters. A single sine reads as a test tone. Two
close low frequencies beat against each other and produce the slow wavering that
sounds like a structure rather than a signal.

On top of that, a slow LFO modulates the gain. The hum breathes. Nothing about
this is complicated, and it's most of the atmosphere.

```ts
const osc = ctx.createOscillator();
osc.type = 'sine';
osc.frequency.value = freq;      // 40 Hz and 63 Hz
// ...summed into a lowpass, gain modulated by a slow LFO
```

## Static is filtered noise, not a noise file

Radio interference is a generated noise buffer through a bandpass filter. The
bandpass is what makes it sound like a radio rather than like white noise: real
interference arrives through a system with a limited frequency response, so
restricting the band is what sells it.

Because it's generated, duration and intensity are parameters at the call site.
`static(ms, gain)` — a short burst on a bad line, a long one when something is
wrong. With a sample you'd be cutting and crossfading a fixed recording to
approximate this.

## Sonar pings and knocking

The ping is a sine burst with an envelope, fed into a delayed gain node for the
echo. Being underwater is entirely in that echo — the delay time and feedback
carry the sense of enclosed volume.

Knocking takes a rhythm array. `knock([0, 180, 420])` plays three impacts at
those offsets, each a filtered transient through a lowpass — knocking on thick
metal has no high end.

Passing rhythm as data rather than picking from recorded variants means the game
can knock in patterns that mean something. Three even knocks read as a person.
The same three knocks slightly irregular read as not-a-person. That distinction
is content, and with samples it would have been two more files.

## The part that justified the whole approach

The game has a fear system. It tracks tension and drives the operator's pulse and
breathing — the heart rate moves from about 58 to 132 beats per minute depending
on what's happening.

That is a continuous parameter. Synthesized audio takes it directly: pulse rate
is a number in the scheduler, breathing rate is another. The player hears
themselves getting tense slightly before they consciously notice it, because the
audio is driven by the same state as the game.

With audio files you would approximate this with three or four pre-recorded loops
and crossfades, and the seams would be audible exactly when the player is most
attentive.

## Real silence is a sound

Worth stating separately: because there is no audio bed playing, silence is
genuinely silent. Cutting the hum entirely is a deliberate effect — five seconds
of nothing in a game that has hummed continuously for twenty minutes is louder
than any sting.

That's only available if the ambience is generated. If your room tone is a looping
file, stopping it is conspicuous in the wrong way.

## The honest downside

You cannot synthesize a voice. Anything requiring recorded human performance is
off the table, and that's a real limit on what the game can do dramatically.

The trade was worth it here because the game is set in a place where every sound
is mechanical, filtered or heard through a radio — which is exactly what
oscillators and filters are good at. On a game that needed convincing organic
sound this would have been the wrong choice.
